# THE SILENT BURDEN
## A Documentary About Male Suicide in the United Kingdom
### Full Script + Interview Guide

**Written by:** Kudzanayi Shepherd Mhlanga
**Version:** 1.0 — June 2026
**Running time:** ~18–20 minutes
**Format:** Short documentary (single film) or 3-episode mini-series

---

> *This documentary deals with suicide and male mental health. If you are struggling, please call Samaritans on 116 123 — free, 24 hours a day, any reason.*

---

## CHARACTER PROFILES

### MARCUS — The Mechanic (38, West Midlands)
Divorced two years ago after a 10-year marriage. Sees his two children every other weekend. Works six days a week at a garage to cover the mortgage on the house his ex-wife and children live in — a choice he made without being asked. Nobody at work knows any of this. He tells people he is fine. He is not fine.

*Core themes: Divorce, fatherhood at a distance, financial pressure, masculine identity through work*

---

### DEREK — The Bus Driver (52, Newcastle, North East England)
Twenty-three years on the same night bus route. Made redundant four months ago when the depot was restructured. His identity was his job — punctual, reliable, the man people could count on. Without that, he does not know who he is. His wife has noticed he barely speaks. He says he is tired. He has not told her about the two weeks last month when he could not get out of bed.

*Core themes: Employment identity, redundancy, middle-age risk, North East deprivation, stoicism*

---

### CALLUM — The Young Professional (24, London)
First in his family to go to university. Works in finance. Calls home every Sunday and tells his mum everything is brilliant. Returns to his flat and sits in the dark. He has never told anyone about the night last November when he stood at the edge of Waterloo Bridge and looked down. He walked home instead. He does not know why. He has not spoken about it since.

*Core themes: Social isolation, performance of success, help-seeking barriers, young men, urban loneliness*

---

### JAMIE — The Veteran (45, Glasgow, Scotland)
Eight years out of the army. Two tours. Does not talk about what he saw. Finds civilian life louder and harder than he expected. Has a group of mates from the regiment he texts occasionally but never actually sees. Drinks most evenings. Not enough to be a problem. Enough to sleep.

*Core themes: PTSD, transition from service, male friendship networks, Scotland's elevated rates, substance use*

---

### LINDA — The Bereaved Mother (61, Swansea, Wales)
Lost her son, Daniel, three years ago. He was 31. A teacher. Kind, funny, popular. She had no idea how bad things had got. She found out afterwards — from his friends, from his journal — that he had been struggling for two years. He never told her. She wants to speak about it because silence, she says, is what took him.

*Core themes: Bereaved family perspective, warning signs, Wales statistics, what help-seeking barriers cost*

# THE SILENT BURDEN

---

### ACT 1 — THE SILENCE [01:30–05:00]

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**

*"Every day, men go to work. They fix engines, drive routes, build cities, write reports, raise children. They provide. They protect. They carry on.*

*And some of them — every single day — are holding on by a thread.*

*They will not tell you. Because they were never taught how. Because the world made clear, very early, that certain things are better kept inside. That asking for help is a kind of failure. That strength means silence.*

*This is the story of what that silence costs."*

---

*[VISUAL: The mechanic — MARCUS — wiping down a workbench. Camera stays at a respectful distance. He does not notice it. Or he does, and does not care.]*

**MARCUS (to camera, seated, plainly):**
*"You just... get on with it. That's what you do. You don't sit around moaning. There's work to be done. Bills to pay. Kids to see at the weekend. You put your head down."*

*[Pause.]*

*"I haven't told my mates about any of it. They don't need that, do they? Blokes don't want to hear it. You'd just be a burden."*

---

*[VISUAL: Derek, sitting in his living room. An empty mug on the table. A framed photo of his old bus on the shelf behind him.]*

**DEREK (quietly):**
*"The job was my identity, wasn't it. Twenty-three years. I knew every road. Every stop. I knew the regulars by name. When they let me go, I didn't know what to do with myself.*

*I kept getting up at five in the morning. Force of habit. My wife thought I was going to interviews. I was just... driving around. Sitting in lay-bys. Killing time until I could go home and pretend the day had meant something."*

---

*[VISUAL: Callum, in a coffee shop. Busy background. He looks well. He is well-dressed. He speaks carefully.]*

**CALLUM:**
*"London is full of people but it's one of the loneliest places I've ever been. You can go a whole weekend without a real conversation. You can be totally fine on the outside — gym, good job, nice flat — and completely falling apart on the inside, and nobody would ever know.*

*I think that's the dangerous part. When the performance is too good."*

---

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
*"In the United Kingdom, three out of every four people who die by suicide are men. Suicide is the leading cause of death for men under fifty. Not cancer. Not heart disease. Not road accidents. Suicide.*

*And yet — this is a crisis that rarely makes the front page.*

*Why?"*

---

### ACT 2 — THE NUMBERS [05:00–09:00]

*[VISUAL: Clean data visualisation sequences — the kind you would build in Power BI. Not clinical. Presented with weight and care.]*

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
*"The data is not hard to find. The Office for National Statistics publishes it every year. Samaritans analyses it. Charities publish it. The numbers are not hidden.*

*The question is whether we are paying attention."*

---

*[VISUAL: Animated bar chart — UK male suicide rate 2000–2024.]*

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
*"In 2024, the male suicide rate in England and Wales reached 17.6 deaths per 100,000 people. The highest rate this century. A rate that has barely changed in 25 years — despite everything we now know about mental health.*

*In Wales, the rate jumped from 22 to 25 per 100,000 in a single year. That is forty-one additional men. Forty-one families."*

---

*[VISUAL: Age breakdown bar chart.]*

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
*"The highest-risk age group is men aged 50 to 54. More than 27 deaths per 100,000. But the fastest-growing concern is younger men — 25 to 34 — where rates have been creeping upward for a decade.*

*Men under 50: more likely to die by suicide than from any other cause.*

*Let that settle."*

---

*[VISUAL: UK nations comparison — Scotland, Wales, Northern Ireland, England by region.]*

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
*"The North East of England — consistently the highest rate in England. Northern Ireland: 20.9 per 100,000. Scotland: 19.3. And Wales — rising.*

*The regions that have been hit hardest by deindustrialisation, by unemployment, by the long decline of the industries that once told men who they were — mining, steel, shipping, fishing — these are the same regions where men are most likely to die by suicide.*

*That is not a coincidence."*

---

*[VISUAL: Deprivation scatter plot — local authority deprivation score vs suicide rate.]*

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
*"The data shows a clear pattern: the more deprived the area, the higher the male suicide rate. Financial stress, unemployment, housing insecurity, social isolation — these are not just hardships. They are risk factors. They are measurable. They are preventable.*

*What happens when men feel they have nowhere to turn?"*

---

*[VISUAL: Clean white screen. Single line of text:]*

*"1 in 8 men in the UK has a common mental health problem. Men are less than half as likely as women to access psychological therapy."*

*[Beat.]*

*"What happens when the system was not built for the way men experience distress?"*

---

### ACT 3 — REAL VOICES [09:00–16:30]

*[VISUAL: Series of honest, direct interviews — BBC documentary style. No dramatic lighting tricks. Just people talking.]*

---

**LINDA (bereaved mother — Swansea):**
*"Daniel was funny. Genuinely funny. He could make anyone laugh. He was a teacher — primary school, Year 4. The children adored him.*

*When the police came to the door, I thought it was a mistake. I actually said to them — I think you've got the wrong house. Because my son was fine. He was absolutely fine.*

*He wasn't fine. He hadn't been fine for two years. I know that now. I couldn't see it then. And the worst part — the part I have to live with — is that he didn't want me to see it. He was protecting me."*

*[She pauses. Looks down. Then back up.]*

*"The silence that you think is kindness. That's what killed my son."*

---

**DR. SARAH OKAFOR (Clinical Psychologist — specialist in male mental health):**
*"The way depression presents in men is often very different to the textbook picture. We are trained to look for sadness, tearfulness, withdrawal from activities. And men do experience those things.*

*But often what you see first in men is irritability. Anger. Risk-taking. Increased alcohol use. Physical complaints — back pain, headaches, fatigue — that don't have a clear physical cause. Men come to the GP with their body, because talking about their mind feels like admitting something.*

*By the time a man presents to a mental health service, he has often been struggling in silence for two to four years. That is two to four years of untreated deterioration."*

---

**MARCUS:**
*"My dad never once talked about feelings. His dad never did. It goes back generations. You fix things. You don't talk about them.*

*I had a week where I didn't want to wake up. I didn't plan anything. I just — didn't want to wake up. And I didn't tell anyone. I went to work. I came home. And I waited for it to pass.*

*[Pause.]*

*It passed. But I keep thinking — what if it hadn't?"*

---

**DEREK:**
*"I looked up the Samaritans number once. I had it on my phone for about a week. I never called it. I kept thinking — I don't deserve to use that. There are people with real problems. I'm just a bloke who lost his job.*

*But that's the thing, isn't it. That's exactly the problem."*

---

**CALLUM:**
*"I stood on Waterloo Bridge for about ten minutes. Just looking down. And then something shifted — I don't even know what — and I walked home.*

*I've never told anyone that before. Not my friends, not my family, nobody. This is the first time I've said it out loud.*

*[Beat.]*

*It feels strange. But it also feels like a relief."*

---

**JAMIE:**
*"In the army, you look after each other. That's everything. You don't leave anyone behind. And then you leave the army, and you're on your own in a way you've never been before. The structure is gone. The brothers are gone. You're just... a civilian. And nobody prepared you for how hard that would be.*

*I'm not in crisis. I want to be clear about that. But I'm not okay either. I'm somewhere in the middle, and I don't quite know how to get out of it."*

---

**RICHARD TAYLOR (Head of People, Manufacturing company):**
*"We had a mental health first aider programme — well-intentioned, tick-box exercise, let's be honest. Three people trained, one poster in the break room. Then we lost a man on the factory floor. He was 44. Nobody saw it coming. Or maybe they did and didn't feel they could say anything.*

*After that, we completely changed the way we approached this. Not one poster. Not one training day. It became part of how we talk to each other, every week, on the shop floor. It became normal to ask — genuinely ask — how someone is doing.*

*The number of men who have come forward since then... it's not nothing. These are men who would never have walked into HR. But they'll talk to a colleague they trust."*

---

**REVEREND KWAME ASANTE (Community leader, Birmingham):**
*"In the communities I serve, there is a particular kind of pressure on men that rarely gets named. The expectation to provide. To be strong. To not show weakness, especially not in front of family. It crosses cultures — it is there in the African communities I know, in the Caribbean communities, in the white working-class communities. Different languages, same silence.*

*Faith communities have a role here. Not to medicalise. Not to preach. Just to provide a place where a man can sit and say — I am not doing well — and be heard without judgment."*

---

### ACT 4 — SOLUTIONS [16:30–19:30]

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
*"This is not inevitable. There are places in this country where things are changing. And the difference, in almost every case, is not expensive. It is not complicated. It is connection. Conversation. And the radical, simple act of asking a man how he is really doing — and staying long enough to hear the honest answer."*

---

*[VISUAL: Andy's Man Club — men sitting in a circle in a community hall. Normal men. Normal space.]*

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
*"Andy's Man Club started with a handful of men in a room in Halifax in 2016. By 2024, it had groups in over 150 locations across the UK. Every Monday evening. Free. Open to any man over 18.*

*No agenda. No therapy. No twelve steps. Just men, talking."*

---

*[VISUAL: Workplace mental health training session.]*

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
*"CALM — the Campaign Against Living Miserably — estimates that two thirds of men they speak to had never talked to anyone about how they were feeling before they called. Not a friend. Not a family member. Not a GP.*

*The phone number is 0800 58 58 58. Five to midnight. Every day."*

---

*[VISUAL: GP consulting room.]*

**DR. SARAH OKAFOR:**
*"We need to make it easier for men to seek help before they are in crisis. That means GPs who are trained to ask the question differently. It means workplaces that build psychological safety. It means friends and family knowing the signs — and knowing that asking directly about suicide does not plant the idea. It does the opposite. It opens a door."*

---

*[VISUAL: Return to the opening scenes — the mechanic, the bus driver, the father, the young professional. The same men. Same spaces. Different feeling. One of them picks up a phone.]*

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
*"The crisis is real. The data proves it.*

*But data does not grieve. Data does not sit with a man at his lowest point and tell him that his life has value. That he is not a burden. That people will not be relieved if he is gone — they will be destroyed.*

*People do that. A phone call does that. A friend who asks the right question — and actually waits for the honest answer — does that.*

*Talking is not weakness. Silence has a cost. And in this country, right now, that cost is being paid in lives."*

---

*[FINAL TITLE CARD — black screen, white text, held for five seconds:]*

**"If you are struggling, you do not have to carry this alone."**

**Samaritans: 116 123 — free, 24 hours, any reason**
**CALM: 0800 58 58 58 — 5pm to midnight**
**Andy's Man Club: andysmanclub.co.uk — Monday evenings, free, everywhere**

*[Fade to black.]*

## INTERVIEW GUIDE
### For Real Contributors (Survivors, Bereaved Families, Experts, Employers)

---

### SECTION A — Men with Lived Experience (Survivors / Those Who Have Struggled)

*Tone: Unhurried, conversational, no pressure to go further than they want to*

1. Can you tell me a little bit about yourself — without going into the difficult stuff yet?
2. When did you first notice that something wasn't right for you? What did that feel like?
3. Was there a specific event, or was it more like a gradual building up of pressure?
4. Did you talk to anyone about how you were feeling at the time? If not — what stopped you?
5. What did you think other people would think if you told them?
6. Were there any moments where things became particularly dangerous? (Only ask if they raise it themselves — do not push)
7. What was the turning point — the moment something shifted?
8. What did you need that you weren't getting?
9. What would you want other men in a similar situation to know right now?
10. Is there anything you wish someone had said to you, or asked you, during that time?

---

### SECTION B — Bereaved Families and Friends

*Tone: Gentle, slow-paced, with space for silence*

1. Can you tell me about [name] — who he was?
2. What do you remember most about him?
3. Looking back, are there things you can see now that you didn't notice at the time?
4. Did he ever talk to you about how he was feeling?
5. What do you think stopped him from asking for help?
6. What do you wish you had known? What would you say to other families who might be in the situation you were in?
7. What do you want people to understand about the impact of this kind of loss?
8. Is there anything about how suicide is discussed publicly that you find frustrating or painful?
9. What would you want to come from a project like this?

---

### SECTION C — Mental Health Professionals and Clinicians

1. From your clinical experience, how does depression typically present in men compared to women?
2. At what point are men usually presenting for help — early, or in crisis?
3. What barriers prevent men from accessing mental health support earlier?
4. What does the data tell us about the groups of men most at risk in the UK?
5. What does the evidence say about what actually works in reducing male suicide rates?
6. What do you wish employers, friends, and family understood about this issue?
7. What needs to change — policy, funding, culture — to make a real difference?

---

### SECTION D — Employers and HR Professionals

1. Has your organisation experienced the loss of an employee to suicide? (Only if they raise it)
2. How does your organisation currently approach mental health in the workplace?
3. What are the most common barriers to men in your workforce seeking support?
4. What has changed in the conversation around men's mental health in your workplace over the last five years?
5. What advice would you give to other managers or HR leaders watching this?
6. What do you think the single most important thing a manager can do is?

---

### SECTION E — Community and Faith Leaders

1. How do the men in your community typically talk about — or avoid talking about — their mental health?
2. What particular pressures do you see men carrying in your community?
3. What role do you think community and faith spaces play in men's wellbeing?
4. What would you say to a man who is struggling but doesn't think he deserves help?

---

### INTERVIEWER NOTES

- Always start with lighter, biographical questions before moving to the difficult material
- Never push a participant beyond what they are comfortable sharing
- Ensure all participants have been given crisis support details before and after the interview
- Give every participant the right to review their section of the script before publication
- Have a mental health support contact available on set at all times
- Consider having a counsellor or support worker present during particularly difficult interviews
