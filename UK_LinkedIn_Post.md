## POST 1 — PROJECT LAUNCH (Week 8, Monday)

June is Men's Mental Health Awareness Month.

And yet — one of the most devastating public health crises affecting men in the United Kingdom still receives far less attention than it deserves.

Three out of every four people who die by suicide in the UK are men.

Suicide is the leading cause of death for men under 50. Not illness. Not accidents. Suicide.

Men are taught, from an early age, to stay strong. To keep going. To provide, solve, and carry on. What often goes unseen are the struggles behind the smile, the weight behind the silence, and the battles being fought behind closed doors — in garages, on night buses, in empty flats, at quiet dinner tables.

I am a Senior Vehicle Engineer with Go Ahead London, transitioning into data analytics. I work with my hands and I am learning to work with data. And I believe that data should do more than support business decisions — data should help us understand human challenges and drive conversations that matter.

This month, I am building a portfolio project focused on male suicide in the United Kingdom. It combines:

— Data analysis of ONS and Samaritans statistics (2000–2024)
— A predictive model exploring which regions face the highest risk
— A documentary script bringing the human reality behind the numbers to life

The project is called **The Silent Burden.**

If you are a mental health professional, researcher, NHS worker, employer, community leader, or someone with lived experience who would be willing to connect or share insight, I would welcome that conversation.

And if you are a man reading this who is quietly carrying something heavy right now — the most important question I can ask is:

**How are you really doing?**

One conversation can change everything.

## POST 2 — DATA FINDINGS (Week 8, Wednesday)

The numbers about male suicide in the United Kingdom are not comfortable. But they need to be heard.

Here is what the data shows:

📊 **17.6 per 100,000** — male suicide rate in England and Wales in 2024. The highest rate this century.

📊 **75%** of all UK suicides are men. This figure has barely changed since the mid-1990s.

📊 **Men aged 50–54** face the highest risk — 27.5 deaths per 100,000.

📊 The **North East of England** has the highest regional rate. Northern Ireland: 20.9. Wales: 25.0 — up sharply in a single year.

📊 Men living in the **most deprived areas** of England are significantly more likely to die by suicide than those in the least deprived.

📊 Men are **less than half as likely** as women to access psychological therapy.

This is not a simple problem. It is the intersection of masculinity norms, economic pressure, social isolation, employment loss, relationship breakdown, and a mental health system that was not designed for the way men experience distress.

But it is also a problem with a data trail. And where there is data, there is understanding. Where there is understanding, there is the possibility of change.

I am analysing this data as part of my portfolio project **The Silent Burden** — and I will be sharing findings over the coming weeks.

If this resonates — share it. Because the first step to changing something is refusing to look away.

## POST 3 — CALL TO ACTION (Week 8, Friday)

Three things I have learned building this project:

**1. The silence is not accidental.**
Men do not struggle silently because they are weak. They struggle silently because the world told them that asking for help was weakness. That is a learned behaviour. It can be unlearned. But it takes someone asking the right question first.

**2. The data points to real places and real people.**
The regions in the UK with the highest male suicide rates are not random. They are the regions that have faced the most economic disruption, the most unemployment, the deepest levels of deprivation. This is not just a mental health issue. It is a social justice issue.

**3. Talking works.**
Andy's Man Club started with a handful of men in a room in Halifax. It now has groups in over 150 locations across the UK. Free. Open. Every Monday. It works because it is simple: men, talking, without judgment.

If you are working in mental health, public health, data analytics, or social impact — and you want to connect, collaborate, or share insight — my inbox is open.

And if you know a man who might be struggling: ask him. Not "are you alright?" — he will say yes. Ask him: "How are you really doing?" And then wait for the honest answer.
